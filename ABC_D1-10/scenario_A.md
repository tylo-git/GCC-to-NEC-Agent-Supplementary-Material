## Comparative Synthesis

> **Executive summary** — GCC generally bars automatic relief for remnant piles unless an Engineer’s written variation unlocks time/cost claims; NEC grants a compensation event if cl. 60.1(12) is met. GCC is Engineer-led and retrospective; NEC is collaborative and prospective, requiring earlier warning, quotation and programme evidence.

<details>
<summary><b>1. Spirit / Philosophy</b> — Risk transfer versus relational governance.</summary>

GCC: The standard GCC allocates unexpected physical-condition risk primarily to the Contractor through deemed site inspection, no-payment, and all-risks tender provisions. Its governance is Engineer-led and claims-oriented: relief is not automatic, but depends on instruction, written variation, notices, particulars, records, and later assessment. In Transaction Cost Economics terms, it emphasises ex ante risk allocation and formal control to reduce opportunistic claims.

NEC: NEC4 ECC treats a qualifying unforeseen physical condition as a compensation event managed through early warning, mitigation, quotation, and implementation. The regime is grounded in mutual trust and co-operation under cl. 10.2, using structured deadlines to force timely information exchange. TCE-wise, NEC relies on relational governance and adaptive processes to reduce the cost of late dispute resolution and maladaptation.

<summary><b>2. GCC Position (Implementation)</b> — Relief depends on Engineer-led variation.</summary>

**Implementation flow:**

1. Contractor discovers remnant piles and notifies Engineer/request instruction if design cannot proceed (Clause 13 / Clause 15) →
2. Engineer may issue written variation to overcome obstruction (Clause 60(1); Clause 61 / Clause 61(1)) →
3. Contractor gives delay notice within 28 days of instruction/event (Clause 50(1)(a)) →
4. Contractor gives cost notice within 28 days after event (Clause 64(2)) →
5. Contractor mitigates, proceeds, records and particularises (Clause 86(1); Clause 64(3); Clause 50(3); Clause 64(4); Clause 64(6)) →
6. Engineer assesses EOT/cost and programme is revised if EOT granted (Clause 50(2); Clause 63; Clause 50(4)).

Under the GCC, the corpus does not use “Project Manager”, “Prices”, or “compensation events”; administration is by the Engineer, time runs against the Time for completion, and payment feeds the Final Contract Sum based on Bills of Quantities. Clause 13(1) deems the Contractor to have inspected the Site. Clause 13(2) bars additional payment for incorrect or insufficient Employer information and does not relieve the Contractor from risk or obligation “on the ground that he did not or could not foresee any matter which may in fact affect or have affected the execution of the Works.” Clause 14 deems Tender rates to cover all risks, liabilities and obligations. Thus remnant piles do not automatically give time or money.

If the obstruction makes the original design physically impossible, the Contractor must seek the Engineer’s instruction under Clause 15. The Engineer may order a written variation under Clause 60(1), such as changed drainage alignment or depth. Only that formal variation unlocks potential relief; valuation is under Clause 61 / Clause 61(1), using Contract rates under Rules 1 and 2 or a fair rate under Rule 3. Clause 50(1)(b)(iv) was cited in the GCC clause set for EOT entitlement.

Notice is strict. For time, the Contractor must give written notice of the cause and probable extent of delay within 28 days of the relevant instruction or event under Clause 50(1)(a). The Engineer determines EOT taking account of all circumstances known to him under Clause 50(2), usually by critical-path and float analysis. If detailed particulars are missing, the Engineer assesses only to the extent possible on available information under Clause 50(3). Whenever EOT is granted, the Contractor revises the programme under Clause 50(4).

For money, Clause 64(2) requires written notice of intention to claim within 28 days after the event. Failure means “such claim shall not be considered” under Clause 64(5). The Contractor must keep “contemporary records as may reasonably be necessary to support any claim” under Clause 64(3), submit a first interim account explaining the sum and calculation under Clause 64(4), and provide particulars; otherwise Clause 64(6) limits assessment to available information. Clause 63 covers actual additional prolongation/disruption costs incurred, excluding profit, not simple extension of Preliminary BQ rates. Clause 86(1) requires the Contractor to proceed with due diligence even during dispute, alongside the mitigation duty.

<summary><b>4. Behavioural Implications</b> — Claims proof shifts to early risk control.</summary>

Under GCC, the Contractor’s first behavioural priority is entitlement preservation. On discovering remnant piles, it should notify the Engineer, request an instruction if the design cannot be built, obtain a written variation, and then serve separate 28-day time and cost notices. Evidence is retrospective and defensive: contemporary labour, plant, obstruction, redesign, disruption and time-related cost records must support Clause 64 claims and Clause 50 delay particulars. The Engineer remains the assessment gatekeeper, determining EOT from known circumstances and valuing variations or actual prolongation cost.

Under NEC, the Contractor’s priority shifts to early transparency and forecast management. It must warn the Project Manager as soon as aware, attend early warning meetings, propose mitigation, notify the compensation event within eight weeks, and price the consequence within three weeks of instruction. Evidence is not merely historic proof; it is also forward-looking Defined Cost, Fee, risk allowance and Accepted Programme impact. The Project Manager must respond within one week to CE notice and within two weeks to the quotation, so administration is time-disciplined.

The incentives differ sharply. GCC late cost notice can bar the claim entirely; inadequate particulars narrow what the Engineer can assess. NEC late CE notice bars changes to Prices and Completion Date, while late early warning reduces recoverable consequences and may affect Disallowed Cost under HK guidance for Options C and D.

<summary><b>5. GCC Lessons for NEC Adoption</b> — GCC routines become NEC management tools.</summary>

Hong Kong practitioners already trained to issue Clause 50(1)(a) delay notices and Clause 64(2) cost notices should transfer that discipline to NEC cl. 15.1 and cl. 61.3. The adjustment is that NEC notice is not only defensive claim protection: early warning is given as soon as the obstruction could increase Prices or delay Completion, while CE notice must follow within eight weeks of becoming aware.

The familiar GCC practice of keeping Clause 64(3) contemporary records, preparing Clause 64(4) interim accounts, and supplying Clause 50(3)/Clause 64(6) particulars transfers to NEC cl. 62.2, cl. 62.3, cl. 63.1 and, for Options C, D, E and F, cl. 52.2. The behavioural change is from proving a historical claim to substantiating a quotation: actual Defined Cost to the dividing date, forecast Defined Cost thereafter, Fee, programme effect and risk allowances.

GCC variation routines under Clause 15, Clause 60(1), Clause 61 and Clause 61(1) remain useful when remnant piles require redesign. Under NEC, the equivalent commercial workflow is the compensation-event quotation and PM assessment process under cl. 61.4, cl. 62.2, cl. 62.3, cl. 64.1, cl. 66.1 and cl. 66.2. The adjustment is to price consequences prospectively and collaboratively rather than wait for final measurement or claim evaluation.

GCC EOT analysis under Clause 50(2), Clause 50(3) and programme revision under Clause 50(4) transfers to NEC Accepted Programme practice under cl. 31.2 and delay assessment under cl. 63.5. In short, GCC claim, record, variation and delay-analysis habits should be used earlier under NEC as risk-management, quotation and mitigation tools.

<summary><b>6. HK-Specific Notes</b> — PAH and DEVB notes shape HK practice.</summary>

For GCC Mega Project Contracts, the Project Administration Handbook (PAH) Chapter 5, Appendix 5.36 prescribes Special Conditions of Contract Clause A4(2). SCC Clause A4(2) expressly writes mitigation into the contract: “The Contractor shall use and continue to use all reasonable endeavours to avoid or reduce the effects of a cause of delay”. It also imposes a stricter reporting duty than the base GCC response alone, requiring the Contractor to submit interim written particulars at intervals of not more than 28 days until the actual delay is ascertainable.

Under Works Bureau Technical Circular WB1900 Appendix B, the contract administrator’s claim powers are constrained. The Architect/Engineer must report all claims and delays to the Employer and must “refer the principles underlying his assessment of each claim... to enable the Employer to provide his view of the matter before the Architect/Engineer/Surveyor reaches a decision.” This reinforces the Hong Kong public-works practice of careful, documented Engineer assessment rather than informal settlement.

For NEC4 ECC in Hong Kong, the DEVB Practice Notes dated July 2023 / December 2024 provide explicit guidance on unforeseen physical conditions. Section A6.3.1.10 reminds Project Managers that cl. 60.3 enforces the “contra proferentem” rule for inconsistencies in Site Information, so ambiguities are interpreted against the Employer/Client who drafted the document. Sections A6.3.1.32 to A6.3.1.35 clarify that compensation-event assessment inherently includes prolongation costs, including extended use of time-related resources; the Contractor must include the effect of prolongation in the quotation, and the evaluation is made by reference to the period when the effect was felt. Those sections reinforce that NEC assessment is prospective, not postponed retrospectively until the end of the contract.

DEVB Practice Note Section A6.3.1.5 reinforces the difference principle in cl. 60.1(12) and cl. 60.2: only the difference between the physical conditions encountered and those for which it would have been reasonable to have allowed is taken into account. The Project Manager must request documentation on the Contractor’s original risk allowances to assess reasonableness. Section A6.1.1.20 warns that if the Contractor fails to give an early warning, the Project Manager will assess the compensation event as if the warning had been given under cl. 63.7; under Options C and D, the Project Manager may even treat costs incurred due to the lack of an early warning as Disallowed Cost.
